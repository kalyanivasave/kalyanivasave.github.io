Product line management

Also called: Product portfolio management

Owning a family of products as a business: which variants to offer, how to price them, when to launch and retire them, and how they perform together.

RelatedSKU rationalizationVariant managementZombie variantsEngineering change (ECR, ECO, ECN)

A product line manager owns the whole family, not one product. In a configurable business that means owning the option space itself: what customers can choose, what each choice costs to keep, and how the line stays easy to buy as it grows.

What the role decides

  • Range: which variants, finishes and options exist, and which go.
  • Price: list prices, option adders, channel and dealer pricing.
  • Lifecycle: launches through new product introduction, changes through engineering change, retirements through phase-out.
  • Performance: volume, margin and complexity cost across the line, not just per product.

Where configuration knowledge helps

Many portfolio decisions look simple on a spreadsheet and turn out to be expensive in the rule set. Adding a fifth finish looks like one row. In practice it's a new column in every price book, new rules wherever finishes are restricted, new renders, new test configurations and new stock. A product line manager who understands the 150% BOM and the configurator can see that cost before it's committed.

The reverse is true too. The configurator's order history is the best portfolio data a company has: which options sell together, which never sell, and which cause fallout. That's where SKU rationalization should start.

Questions people ask

How is product line management different from product management?

In manufacturing, a product manager often owns one product or platform; a product line manager owns a family and the trade-offs between its members.

What data does a product line manager need from configuration?

Option-level volumes, attach rates (which options are bought together), fallout by option, and the maintenance effort each option creates.
