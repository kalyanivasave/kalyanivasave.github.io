Modular product architecture

Also called: Modularisation, platform design

Designing a product family from a limited set of shared modules with standard interfaces, so variety comes from combining modules rather than from new parts.

RelatedVariant managementCombinatorial explosionConfigure to order (CTO)150% BOM

Modularity is how a product family can offer thousands of variants from a few hundred parts. The variety lives in the combinations, not in the components.

How it works

The product is split into modules (a base, a seat, arms, a back), each with a few variants and a standard interface to its neighbours. As long as every arm fits every seat through the same interface, adding a new arm adds one module, not one new chair per existing combination.

What it means for configuration

Good modularity makes the configurator's job easier: constraints concentrate at the interfaces, and the 150% BOM becomes a tidy set of module choices. Poor modularity shows up as rules that read like exceptions: this arm with that seat only in these finishes. When a rule set is full of exceptions, the architecture is usually the cause.

Questions people ask

Is modular always better?

No. Modularity costs some performance, weight or aesthetics at the interfaces. Premium products sometimes trade modularity away on purpose.

Who decides the module boundaries?

Engineering and product management together. Configuration should be in the room, because the boundaries decide how many rules will be needed for the next ten years.
