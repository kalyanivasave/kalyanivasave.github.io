Combinatorial explosion

Also called: Variant explosion

The way the number of possible product variants multiplies, rather than adds, with every new option, until it outgrows anyone's ability to test or stock it.

RelatedVariant managementConstraintSKU rationalizationConfigure to order (CTO)

Options don't add. They multiply. A chair with 3 frame colours and 4 fabrics is not 7 choices; it's 12 chairs. Add 2 arm types and it's 24. Add 5 casters and it's 120. Nobody decided to offer 120 chairs. It happened one reasonable meeting at a time.

This is how a single office chair ends up with more than a million possible configurations. Infor's case study on Herman Miller puts the Aeron at more than 1.2 million permutations. More on that in A chair with 1.2 million versions.

Why it matters

  • Testing. You cannot test a million configurations. You test the rules, with carefully chosen test configurations, and trust the model.
  • Inventory. You cannot stock a million variants. You stock parts, and assemble to order.
  • Pricing. You cannot price a million variants one by one. You price options and let a build-up do the arithmetic.

Taming it

constraints cut the space down to what can actually be built. Modular architecture keeps the number of parts far smaller than the number of variants. SKU rationalization removes options whose volume no longer pays for their complexity. The goal isn't fewer variants. It's variants that cost less to have.

Questions people ask

Is a million configurations a problem?

Not by itself. It's a problem if each one needs its own part number, drawing or test. If they are all assembled from a few hundred shared parts by a sound rule set, a million configurations can be perfectly healthy.

How do you know which configurations matter?

Look at order history. In most configurable portfolios a small share of configurations carries most of the volume, and a long tail is ordered rarely or never.
