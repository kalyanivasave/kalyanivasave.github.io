My work spans access control devices at Reliance Jio, HVAC and power equipment at Lumina Datamatics, and configure-to-order and make-to-order modular office space product lines across 7 brands at Herman Miller, the company behind the Eames Lounge Chair and the Aeron, which has ~1.2 million product configurations.

At Herman Miller I also worked on the decisions that shape a product line: which SKUs stay orderable from launch to end of life, how dealer pricing changes, and how to get new products to market faster. We cut time to market by 22%. What interests me most is the whole portfolio: which variants earn their place, which quietly cost more than they sell, and how a product line stays easy to buy as it grows.

Featured project

Configuration Portfolio Cockpit

See all labs

A product line review for a real manufacturer. I took Lancer Worldwide's 21 current beverage dispensers and built the tool I'd want in the room when a product line team decides what stays in the price book.

It ranks every configuration by revenue, charges each one for the unique parts only it uses, and recommends whether to quick-ship, keep, reprice, restrict, merge, phase out or retire it. Then it tests the configuration rules behind those products against real part numbers from Lancer's public operation manuals.

  • 41 configurations across all 21 current products, ranked A, B and C by revenue
  • Seven possible decisions, from quick-ship to retire, each with its reasons and the checks to make first
  • Configurators for three product families built from real part numbers in Lancer's public manuals, with regression tests
  • A decision log with announce, last order and last ship dates for the review board

Volumes, prices and costs are practice data. Part numbers and install rules are Lancer's own, from public documents.

Glossary

14 full entries and 33 quick definitions, written the way I'd explain them to a new analyst on day one.

Open the glossary

Configure to order (CTO)

A strategy where the product is engineered in advance as options and modules, and a specific variant is only assembled once a customer has chosen it.

150% BOM

One bill of materials that holds every part any variant of a product family could need, with a condition on each line that decides when it is used.

Variant management

The discipline of deciding which product variants a company offers, how they are defined, and how their cost and complexity are kept under control over their whole life.

Combinatorial explosion

The way the number of possible product variants multiplies, rather than adds, with every new option, until it outgrows anyone's ability to test or stock it.

SKU rationalization

A periodic review that removes products or options whose volume, margin or strategic value no longer justifies the cost of keeping them.

Zombie variants

Options or variants that almost nobody orders but that everyone still has to maintain, because nobody has formally retired them.

How I think

A few things I believe about configurable products

My depth is in configuration rules, BOM structure and change control. I'm not an expert in every corner of PLM, and these are positions, not laws. Tell me where you'd push back.

If it can be quoted, it must be buildable

A configurator is a promise. Every constraint exists so the quote, the MBOM and the routing agree before the order reaches the plant.

Every option has a running cost

Adding a variant takes a meeting. Keeping it costs price-book columns, rules, tests and stock for years. Portfolios should be pruned as deliberately as they grow.

An ECO isn't done when it's approved

It's done when effectivity, stock disposition and every open order on the where-used list have been accounted for.

Fix the rule, not the order

Fallout is a defect report on the rule set. Correct the order, then fix the constraint and save the failure as a test configuration.
