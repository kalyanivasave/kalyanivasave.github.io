Glossary

The vocabulary of configurable products, written the way I'd explain it to a new analyst on day one. 14 full entries for the ideas that matter most, and 33 quick definitions for everything else. If you'd define something differently, tell me; I'd rather be corrected than consistent.

Full entries

Definition, how it works, where it goes wrong, examples

150% BOM

One bill of materials that holds every part any variant of a product family could need, with a condition on each line that decides when it is used.

Combinatorial explosion

The way the number of possible product variants multiplies, rather than adds, with every new option, until it outgrows anyone's ability to test or stock it.

Configure to order (CTO)

A strategy where the product is engineered in advance as options and modules, and a specific variant is only assembled once a customer has chosen it.

Constraint

A rule that says which combinations of options are allowed, required or impossible.

CPQ

Software that lets salespeople or dealers configure a product, price it and turn it into a quote, then hand the won order to ERP.

Effectivity

The rule that says from when, or from which unit, a change applies, so old and new versions can coexist without breaking orders in flight.

Engineering change (ECR, ECO, ECN)

The controlled process for changing a released product: requesting the change, approving it with its effectivity and impact, and telling everyone affected.

Modular product architecture

Designing a product family from a limited set of shared modules with standard interfaces, so variety comes from combining modules rather than from new parts.

Order fallout

Orders that can't flow straight through to production because something in them is invalid, unpriced, unbuildable or unclear, and needs a person to fix it.

Product configurator

Software that holds a product's option model and rules, guides a user to a valid combination, and produces the BOM, routing and price for it.

Product line management

Owning a family of products as a business: which variants to offer, how to price them, when to launch and retire them, and how they perform together.

SKU rationalization

A periodic review that removes products or options whose volume, margin or strategic value no longer justifies the cost of keeping them.

Variant management

The discipline of deciding which product variants a company offers, how they are defined, and how their cost and complexity are kept under control over their whole life.

Zombie variants

Options or variants that almost nobody orders but that everyone still has to maintain, because nobody has formally retired them.

Quick definitions

100% BOMAlso: Order BOM, resolved BOM

The bill of materials for one specific configuration: exactly the parts needed to build what this customer ordered, nothing optional left in it.

Example. A desk ordered with a 60 in top, a height-adjustable base and a power module resolves to one top, one base, one module and one hardware kit. That list is its 100% BOM.

150% BOMAlso: Generic BOM, super BOM, overloaded BOM

A single bill of materials that holds every part any configuration of a product family could need, each line carrying a condition that says when it applies. Configuring a product means filtering the 150% BOM down to a 100% BOM.

Example. A lock family's 150% BOM lists both the mechanical and the electrified chassis. Choosing “fail-secure” keeps the electrified one and drops the other. The Rules Lab shows this live.

Read the full entry

Attribute and optionAlso: Feature and value, characteristic

An attribute is a choice the customer makes; options are the allowed answers. The pair is the basic vocabulary of every configurator.

Example. Finish is an attribute. Satin chrome, satin stainless and matte black are its options.

Bill of materials (BOM)

The structured list of parts, sub-assemblies and quantities needed to make a product. Multi-level BOMs nest sub-assemblies inside assemblies.

Example. A chair BOM lists a seat assembly, which has its own BOM of foam, shell and fabric.

Change impact analysisAlso: Impact assessment

Working out, before a change is approved, everything it will touch: parent assemblies, configured products, open orders, stock, suppliers, documents and downstream systems.

Example. Replacing one bracket looks small until where-used shows it sits in 40 assemblies and 300 open orders.

CMFAlso: Colour, material, finish

The surface and material choices of a product, managed as master data. CMF often drives the largest number of variants in furniture and building products.

Example. One chair in 12 fabrics, 4 frame finishes and 3 base materials is 144 CMF combinations before any functional option.

Compatibility constraintAlso: Configuration constraint

A rule that says which options can or cannot be combined. Constraint-based modelling describes a product by its constraints instead of listing every valid combination.

Example. “A fire-labelled door cannot use the curved lever” is a compatibility constraint.

Read the full entry

Configuration ruleAlso: Business rule, product rule

Any logic in a configurator: constraints that block invalid choices, defaults, rules that select BOM lines, and rules that add price.

Example. If the function is Passage, set the cylinder to None and hide the key options.

Configure-to-order (CTO)

A strategy where the product is assembled from pre-designed modules and options only after the customer configures and orders it. Engineering is done in advance; the order just picks a combination.

Example. Office chairs, door hardware and many HVAC units are configure-to-order.

Read the full entry

CPQAlso: Configure, price, quote

Software that lets sales teams or dealers configure a product, price it and produce a quote. CPQ sits on top of the configuration rules and pricing, and hands the order to ERP.

Example. A dealer builds a quote for 200 workstations in CPQ; when it is won, the order flows to ERP with each configuration intact.

Read the full entry

Decision table

A table that writes a rule as rows of conditions and outcomes, so people can read, review and test it without reading code.

Example. Rows of function × cylinder with the outcome Allowed or Blocked. The Rules tab of the Rules Lab is a simple version.

EBOM and MBOMAlso: Engineering BOM, manufacturing BOM

The EBOM describes the product as engineering designed it. The MBOM describes it as the plant builds it, with the assembly sequence, packaging and consumables added. Translating one into the other is a core configuration job.

Example. Engineering lists “screw, M6” once; the MBOM lists 8 of them at the station where they are driven.

ECNAlso: Engineering change notice

The communication that tells everyone affected that a change is released: plant, purchasing, sales, service and dealers.

Example. A notice to dealers that the old bracket is no longer orderable as a spare after the cut-in date.

Read the full entry

ECOAlso: Engineering change order

The approved change, with what changes, from when (effectivity), and what happens to existing stock and open orders.

Example. Release bracket P-1042 rev C from 1 December; use up rev B stock on open orders.

Read the full entry

ECRAlso: Engineering change request

A formal request to change a part, drawing or product. It states the problem, the proposed change and the reason, and starts the review.

Example. “Bracket P-1042 cracks in testing; propose thicker steel.”

Read the full entry

EffectivityAlso: Date effectivity, serial effectivity

The rule for when a change takes effect: from a date, a serial number or a lot. Effectivity lets old and new versions coexist in the system without breaking open orders.

Example. Orders dated before 1 December get the rev A lever; orders from that day get rev B.

Read the full entry

End of lifeAlso: Obsolescence, phase-out

Retiring a part, option or product: stop selling it, use up or scrap stock, keep service parts as required, and remove it from the configurator on the right date.

Example. Oil-rubbed bronze can be ordered until 31 December, and is blocked from 1 January.

Engineer-to-order (ETO)

Products designed, at least partly, for each order. ETO needs engineering work after the sale; CTO does not.

Example. A custom conveyor line for one warehouse is engineer-to-order.

Make-to-order (MTO)

Production starts only when an order arrives, as opposed to make-to-stock, where finished goods are built ahead of demand. CTO is a form of make-to-order.

Example. A configured desk is built after the order; a box of standard screws is made to stock.

Master dataAlso: Product master data

The reference data every transaction depends on: part numbers, descriptions, units, lifecycle states, CMF, prices. Wrong master data quietly corrupts every order that uses it.

Example. A wrong unit of measure (each instead of pair) doubles every lever in every order.

NPIAlso: New product introduction

The process of bringing a new product from design release to first production and sale: BOMs, routings, rules, pricing, documentation and launch readiness.

Example. A new chair can be engineered and still not be sellable until its rules, prices and dealer content are live.

Order falloutAlso: Order exceptions, rejected orders

Orders that cannot flow straight through to production because something in them is invalid, unpriced, unbuildable or unclear. Each one needs a person to fix it, and each one is a signal. More in this deep dive.

Example. A quote for a black lock on a thick door reaches the plant, but black is not made in the long spindle.

Read the full entry

OrderabilityAlso: SKU orderability, sellable status

Whether a part, option or SKU may be ordered right now, and by whom. Orderability is controlled by lifecycle state and must match what the plant can supply.

Example. A superseded part stays in the system for service history but is blocked from new orders.

Phase-in and phase-outAlso: Cut-in, cut-over

Introducing a replacement and retiring the old version in a planned overlap, usually controlled by effectivity and stock levels.

Example. The new lever phases in on 1 December while the old one is used up on orders already placed.

Product configuratorAlso: Configurator, configuration engine

The software that holds the product model, applies the rules as a user chooses options, and produces a valid configuration, BOM and price.

Example. The Infor LN Product Configurator (PCF) is the configurator behind many Infor manufacturers' orders.

Read the full entry

Revision control

Keeping every version of a part or document identified (rev A, rev B), with only the right revision released and used at any time.

Example. Rev B of a drawing is released; rev A is kept for history but cannot be used to build.

RoutingAlso: Operation routing

The sequence of operations, work centres and standard times needed to build an item. Configurable products often have configurable routings too.

Example. An electrified lock gets an extra electrical test operation that a mechanical lock skips.

Smart part numberAlso: Configuration code, model code

A part number whose characters encode the chosen options, so a configuration can be read from its code.

Example. NL7100-OF-L1-626-STD-RH-175-N-N reads as office function, straight lever, satin chrome, standard cylinder, right hand.

Test configurationAlso: Rule test case

A saved configuration with a known correct outcome, re-run after every rule change to prove nothing broke. A set of them is the configurator's regression test.

Example. “A passage lock with a key must be rejected” is one test configuration among many.

UATAlso: User acceptance testing

Testing by the people who will use a change (sales, dealers, order entry) before it goes live, to confirm it works for real orders.

Example. A dealer team quotes ten real projects in the new rule set before release.

VariantAlso: Product variant

One distinct buildable version of a product, defined by a specific set of options.

Example. Two locks that differ only in handing are two variants.

Where-usedAlso: Where-used report

A search that starts from a part and finds every assembly, product and configuration that contains it. It is the first step of most impact analyses.

Example. Where-used on a spindle shows every lock family and door thickness that uses it.

Zombie variant

An option nobody buys any more but everyone still has to maintain: it keeps its rules, prices, data and test cases alive. The term comes from Julian Weyer.

Example. A finish that sold three units last year but still adds a column to every price list.
